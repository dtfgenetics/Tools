Root Rot & Damage

Root damage can create yellowing, drooping, stalled growth, necrosis, and nutrient-like symptoms. The goal is to verify root-zone stress before making aggressive feed corrections.

Root OxygenRoot-Zone Diagnostics

What to learn

  • Damaged roots reduce water and nutrient uptake.
  • Warning signs can include poor branching, dark/slimy roots, off smell, slow recovery, and persistent wilt.
  • Root stress can follow saturation, heat, cold, pests, pathogens, compaction, or salt buildup.
  • Leaf symptoms alone cannot confirm root rot.
  • Verify progression and environment before acting.
Rule: treat roots as a possible cause when leaf issues do not match feed changes.

Visual reference slot

Teaching schematic, not a diagnostic photograph. Substrate staining can change root color. Compare root tip formation, firmness, recent growth, moisture and the condition of affected plants. Suspected pathogens require independent verification.

Differentiate damage from infection

Dark or stained roots, slow growth and drooping are not proof of a pathogen. Old roots may look different from new roots, and some nutrient solutions stain otherwise functional tissues.

  • Physical: investigate compaction, mechanical injury, poor drainage and persistent saturation.
  • Chemical: inspect EC, pH, temperature and recent changes in solution or amendments.
  • Biological: pests and microorganisms can damage roots, but species-level identification requires appropriate confirmation.

Five-step field examination

  1. Photograph whole-plant symptoms and record when changes began.
  2. Check medium, moisture, drainage and root-zone temperature before disturbing the root ball.
  3. Examine accessible tips gently for firmness and active branching; document odor and discoloration without diagnosing from them.
  4. Measure medium-appropriate pH and EC; inspect irrigation and recent spray or feeding changes.
  5. Recheck progression and obtain expert/laboratory confirmation where a specific pathogen diagnosis would alter treatment.

Measurements to pair

Substrate moisture, root-zone temperature, smell, root color, media drainage, pH, EC, irrigation frequency, oxygen/aeration notes, and symptom progression.

Root Rot

Root rot is a cluster of water mould and fungal infections that exploit oxygen-depleted, warm, or stressed root zones. Prevention is far more effective than treatment — once established, root rot is very difficult to fully reverse.

← DrybackModule IndexModule IndexRoot Zone Diagnostics →
Core rule

Root rot requires three conditions simultaneously: pathogen, stressed roots, and moisture.

Pythium and Fusarium exist in almost every grow environment. They become problems when root stress (oxygen deprivation, temperature stress, or damage) and sustained moisture combine. Remove any one condition and progression stops.

Prevention over treatment

A plant that has lost 40%+ of its root mass to rot will struggle to recover.

Root hairs destroyed by pythium do not regenerate quickly. Correct oxygen levels, temperature, and drainage is far more effective than any treatment protocol applied after the fact.

Root zone pathogens and predisposing conditions

Primary root rot pathogens

water mould

Pythium spp.

Produces zoospores that swim toward root exudates in saturated substrate. Most common cause of root rot in cannabis. Thrives at 20–26°C with high moisture and low oxygen. Slimy, brown roots with fishy smell.

water mould

Phytophthora spp.

Closely related to Pythium; similar infection mechanism. Causes crown rot at the stem base as well as root rot. Brown lesions at soil line with water-soaked appearance.

fungal

Fusarium oxysporum

Can colonise the vascular system — causing wilt that presents as sudden collapse even with apparently healthy roots. Brown vascular discolouration in stem cross-section is diagnostic.

fungal

Rhizoctonia solani

Most destructive at seedling stage — causes damping off. Brown, shrunken lesions at the crown and on lateral roots. Also causes root rot in older plants under stress.

Response protocol

Identification

Confirm before acting.

  1. Remove plant from container at lights-off — root inspection without light stress
  2. Observe root colour: brown, grey, or black vs white/cream healthy
  3. Squeeze a root: healthy = firm; infected = slimy, breaks easily
  4. Smell the root mass: fishy, rotten, or sour = active anaerobic pathogen
  5. Check stem base for brown lesions — indicates Phytophthora or Rhizoctonia
Response sequence

Once confirmed.

  1. Improve drainage and reduce irrigation frequency immediately
  2. Improve aeration — raise dissolved oxygen in hydro; allow dryback in coco/soil
  3. Reduce root zone temperature below 22°C — slows pathogen reproduction
  4. Remove and dispose of heavily infected plants to prevent spread
  5. Avoid hydrogen peroxide — sterilises beneficial microbes that suppress pythium
  6. Consider Trichoderma and Bacillus subtilis after environment is corrected
